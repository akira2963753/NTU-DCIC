\section{(a) Root-$u$ Zadoff-Chu Sequence}

\question{Use the prime sequence length $N = 53$. Let $u = 1 + (m \bmod 52)$, where $m$ is the last two digits of
the student ID. For odd $N$, define the root-$u$ Zadoff-Chu sequence as
\[ x_u[n] = e^{-j\pi u n(n+1)/N}, \quad n = 0, \ldots, N-1. \]
Generate $x_u[n]$. Plot its real part, imaginary part, magnitude, and unwrapped phase. (10\%)}

With $u = 40$ and $N = 53$, I compute $x_{40}[n]$ straight from the definition. $\gcd(40, 53) = 1$, so 40 is a
valid root. The first samples are $x_{40}[0] = 1$, $x_{40}[1] = 0.0296 + 0.9996j$ and
$x_{40}[2] = -0.0888 - 0.9960j$.

The magnitude panel is flat: $|x_u[n]| = 1$ for every $n$, up to a rounding error of $1.1 \times 10^{-16}$. All the
information sits in the phase. Constant amplitude plus the zero autocorrelation shown in (b) is what people call a
CAZAC sequence, and it is the reason LTE uses ZC sequences for PSS and PRACH.

The real and imaginary parts are also mirror images about $n = 26$. This is easy to prove. Since
$(N-1-n)(N-n) = n(n+1) + N(N - 1 - 2n)$, we have $x_u[N-1-n] = x_u[n]\,e^{-j\pi u (N-1-2n)}$, and $N - 1 - 2n$ is even,
so $x_u[N-1-n] = x_u[n]$.

The unwrapped phase needs a comment, because it does not look like the parabola $\phi[n] = -\pi u n(n+1)/N$ in the
definition. That parabola drops to $-6534.5$\,rad at $n = 52$, and its step from one sample to the next is
$-2\pi u(n+1)/N$, which is already larger than $\pi$ at $n = 0$. MATLAB \texttt{unwrap} assumes each step is in
$(-\pi, \pi]$, so it cannot follow the parabola. It picks the path with the smallest steps instead, which stays between
0 and 13.2\,rad and keeps the same mirror symmetry. The two paths differ only by multiples of $2\pi$ (the residue after
wrapping is at most $9.5 \times 10^{-13}$), so they describe exactly the same samples. The bottom panel shows this
directly: after adding the right multiple of $2\pi$ to each unwrapped sample, every point lands on the analytic
parabola $-\pi u n(n+1)/N$. In other words, the chirp is
aliased by the sampling. The same fact explains why root $u$ and root $u - N = -13$ give the same sequence:
$e^{-j\pi(u-N)n(n+1)/N} = x_u[n]\,e^{j\pi n(n+1)} = x_u[n]$, because $n(n+1)$ is always even.

\section{(b) Periodic and Linear Autocorrelation}

\question{Calculate and stem-plot the normalized periodic autocorrelation for $l = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$. (10\%)
Also calculate a nonperiodic linear autocorrelation and explain why it does not exhibit the same zero-sidelobe
property. (10\%)
\[ R_u[l] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_u[n]\,x_u^*[(n-l) \bmod N], \qquad
   R_u^{\text{lin}}[l] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=l}^{N-1} x_u[n]\,x_u^*[n-l]. \]}

\textbf{Periodic autocorrelation.} I get $R_u[0] = 1$ and $\max_{l \neq 0}|R_u[l]| = 7.9 \times 10^{-14}$.
The small residue comes from rounding the large phase arguments, so all 52 sidelobes are zero.

To see why, multiply a ZC sample by a shifted copy. The $n^2$ terms cancel and the phase of the product is
\emph{linear} in $n$:
\begin{equation}
    x_u[n]\,x_u^*[n-l]
    = e^{-j\pi u [\,n(n+1) - (n-l)(n-l+1)\,]/N}
    = \underbrace{e^{-j\pi u(l - l^2)/N}}_{c_l}\; e^{-j2\pi u l n/N}.
\end{equation}
This still holds with the cyclic index $(n-l) \bmod N$. Replacing $n-l$ by $n-l+N$ changes the phase by
$\pi u (2n - 2l + N + 1)$, and with $N$ odd that is a multiple of $2\pi$. So
\begin{equation}
    R_u[l] = \frac{c_l}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} e^{-j2\pi u l n/N} = c_l\,\delta[l],
\end{equation}
since for $0 < l < N$ the product $ul$ is not a multiple of the prime $N$, and a complex exponential summed over whole
periods gives zero.

\textbf{Linear autocorrelation.} The summand is the same, but the sum now runs over $n = l, \ldots, N-1$ only, which is
$N - l$ terms instead of a full period. The geometric series does not close, and
\begin{equation}
    |R_u^{\text{lin}}[l]| = \frac{1}{N}\left|\frac{\sin\big(\pi u l (N-l)/N\big)}{\sin(\pi u l/N)}\right|, \quad l \neq 0 .
\end{equation}
which is generally not zero. The simulated values agree with this formula to $3.9 \times 10^{-14}$ (black circles in the
figure). The largest sidelobe is $|R_u^{\text{lin}}[8]| = 0.1296$, or $-17.7$\,dB, and the average sidelobe is $0.0278$.

So the zero sidelobes depend on the wrap-around. In the periodic sum, the samples that slide off the end of the shifted
copy reappear at the start, and the $N$ terms form a full set of $N$-th roots of unity. The linear sum loses $l$ of those
terms, and what is left does not cancel. LTE handles this with a cyclic prefix in front of the ZC sequence, which makes
the receiver's linear correlation behave like a periodic one.

\end{figure}

\section{(c) Periodic Cross-Correlation}

\question{Choose $v = 1 + ((u + 16) \bmod 52)$. Calculate the periodic cross-correlation $C_{u,v}[l]$ for
$l = 0, 1, \ldots, N-1$. (10\%)
\[ C_{u,v}[l] = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{n=0}^{N-1} x_u[n]\,x_v^*[(n-l) \bmod N] \]}

With $u = 40$ and $v = 5$, the magnitude of the cross-correlation does not depend on the lag:
\begin{equation}
    |C_{40,5}[l]| = 0.137361 = \frac{1}{\sqrt{53}} \quad \text{for all } l = 0, \ldots, 52 .
\end{equation}
The minimum and maximum agree to six decimals. Only the phase changes with $l$ (bottom panel).

This time the $n^2$ terms do not cancel. The phase of $x_u[n]x_v^*[n-l]$ is $-\pi(u - v)n^2/N$ plus a term linear in
$n$, which makes $C_{u,v}[l]$ a quadratic Gauss sum. When $N$ is prime and $\gcd(u - v, N) = \gcd(35, 53) = 1$, such a
sum always has magnitude $\sqrt{N}$, so $|C_{u,v}[l]| = \sqrt{N}/N = 1/\sqrt{N}$. A flat level cannot be any lower than
this, because Parseval requires $\sum_l |C_{u,v}[l]|^2 = 1$ for unit-energy sequences (here $53 \times 1/53 = 1$).
The practical meaning is that a wrong root never looks like a weak copy of the right one. Its correlation is spread
evenly over all lags, with no peak anywhere.

$\tau = \tau_0$ and zero at every other delay, so the peak is a single stem and the delay is unambiguous.

absolute value. In an LTE receiver doing PSS or PRACH detection, the root would tell which cell or preamble was sent and
the delay would give the timing. Noise would raise the flat floor somewhat, but with a 17\,dB margin the decision
should hold well into low SNR.

\textbf{Result.} Both periodic autocorrelations take only two values, the peak at $k = 0$ and one constant sidelobe:
\begin{equation}
    \Phi_s(k) =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & k = 0 \\
        -1/7, & k \neq 0
    \end{cases} \quad (N = 7),
    \qquad
    \Phi_s(k) =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & k = 0 \\
        +1/13, & k \neq 0
    \end{cases} \quad (N = 13).
\end{equation}

\textbf{Why.} Barker codes are defined through the \emph{aperiodic} autocorrelation
$A(k) = \sum_{n=k}^{N-1} S(n)S(n-k)$, with every sidelobe satisfying $|A(k)| \le 1$. For odd $N$ the sidelobes are 0 at
odd $k$ and $\pm 1$ at even $k$, as the second row of each table shows. The periodic sum is the aperiodic one plus the
part that wraps around:
\begin{equation}
    N\Phi_s(k) = A(k) + A(N - k).
\end{equation}
With $N$ odd, $k$ and $N - k$ always have opposite parity. One of the two terms is therefore 0 and the other is
$\pm 1$, which is why every periodic sidelobe comes out the same. In the length-7 code the even-$k$ sidelobes are all
$-1$, giving $-1/7$. In the length-13 code they are all $+1$, giving $+1/13$.

\textbf{Sum check.} Adding up all shifts gives $\sum_k N\Phi_s(k) = \big(\sum_n S(n)\big)^2$. For $N = 7$, $\sum S = -1$
and $7 + 6 \times (-1) = 1 = (-1)^2$. For $N = 13$, $\sum S = -5$ and $13 + 12 \times 1 = 25 = (-5)^2$. Put another way,
a two-valued result forces the sidelobe to be $\big((\sum S)^2 - N\big)/\big(N(N-1)\big)$, and the script gets this
value for both codes.

The periodic sidelobes are only $1/N$ in magnitude, so a Barker code still has one sharp peak when it repeats.
That is what makes it useful as a synchronization word; IEEE 802.11b uses the 11-chip version for this.

\chapter{Q3 — m-Sequences and Gold Code}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────
The octal pair $103_8$ and $147_8$ gives
\begin{equation}
    103_8 = (1\,000\,011)_2 \;\Rightarrow\; p(x) = x^6 + x + 1, \qquad
    147_8 = (1\,100\,111)_2 \;\Rightarrow\; p'(x) = x^6 + x^5 + x^2 + x + 1 .
\end{equation}

\textbf{LFSR connection.} I wire the registers as in the spec's figure. Each one has six stages, numbered 1 on the
left to 6 on the right. On each clock, the XOR of the tapped stages goes into stage 1, everything shifts one stage to
the right, and stage 6 is the output. A stage $k$ is tapped when $x^k$ is in the polynomial, which matches the arrows in
the figure:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=1.5em]
    \item top LFSR, $p(x) = x^6 + x + 1$, taps at stages 1 and 6 (two arrows);
    \item bottom LFSR, $p'(x) = x^6 + x^5 + x^2 + x + 1$, taps at stages 1, 2, 5 and 6 (four arrows).
\end{itemize}
The generator output is the XOR of the two stage-6 outputs.

\textbf{Initial states.} I write $r_1 = 20$ and $r_2 = 30$ in six-bit binary and put the MSB in stage 1, which gives
$r_1 \to \texttt{010100}$ and $r_2 \to \texttt{011110}$. Neither is all zeros, so both registers will run.

\section{(b) Periodic Autocorrelation}

\question{Map a binary sequence $s_i[n]$ to $d_i[n] = (-1)^{s_i[n]}$. For both sequences, verify the normalized periodic
autocorrelation for all 63 lags. (20\%)
\[ R_i[l] = \frac{1}{63}\sum_{n=0}^{62} d_i[n]\,d_i[(n+l) \bmod 63] \]}

For both sequences the simulation gives exactly
\begin{equation}
    R_1[l] = R_2[l] =
    \begin{cases}
        1, & l = 0 \\
        -1/63 = -0.0159, & l = 1, \ldots, 62 .
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}
This is the two-valued autocorrelation expected of an m-sequence, and it comes from two properties:
\begin{enumerate}[label=\arabic*)]
    \item Shift-and-add. For $l \neq 0$, $s[n] \oplus s[n+l]$ satisfies the same linear recurrence as $s[n]$, so it
    is another cyclic shift $s[n+k]$ of the same sequence. In $\pm 1$ form this reads $d[n]\,d[n+l] = d[n+k]$. The
    script confirms it for all 62 nonzero lags of $d_1$.
    \item Balance. One period has 32 ones and 31 zeros, so $\sum_n d[n] = 31 - 32 = -1$.
\end{enumerate}
Putting them together, $63\,R[l] = \sum_n d[n+k] = -1$ for every $l \neq 0$. At full scale the $-1/63$ sidelobes sit
almost on the zero line, so the figure adds a zoomed panel under each sequence; all 62 sidelobes lie exactly on
$-1/63$.

\section{(d) Is the Answer in (c) Correct?}

\question{Please judge if your answer in (c) is correct or not? Please state the reason. (5\%)}

\textbf{Yes, the answer in (c) is correct.} I checked it in five ways.

\textbf{1) Gold's theorem.} $p(x)$ and $p'(x)$ are a preferred pair of degree $n = 6$. For a preferred pair, the
periodic cross-correlation between the two m-sequences, or between any two codewords of the Gold family they generate,
can only be
\begin{equation}
    \frac{1}{N}\left\{-t(n),\ -1,\ t(n) - 2\right\}, \qquad
    t(n) = 1 + 2^{\lfloor (n+2)/2 \rfloor} = 1 + 2^{4} = 17 ,
\end{equation}
i.e.\ $\{-17, -1, 15\}/63$, which is the set I got for $C_{1,3}$ and $C_{2,3}$.

\textbf{2) How often each value appears.} $C_{1,3}$ has to look like $C_{1,2}$. Using $d_3 = d_1 d_2$,
\begin{equation}
    63\,C_{1,3}[l] = \sum_n \underbrace{d_1[n]\,d_1[n-l]}_{=\,d_1[n-k] \text{ for } l \neq 0}\, d_2[n-l],
\end{equation}
so for $l \neq 0$ it equals the cross-correlation of $d_1$ and $d_2$ at some other lag, and at $l = 0$ it is
$\sum_n d_2[n] = -1$. $C_{2,3}$ works the same way. For a preferred pair with $n = 6$ (decimation $q = 2^k + 1 = 5$,
$e = \gcd(n, k) = 2$), the theory also says how many lags give each value:
\begin{equation}
    -17\!: 2^{n-e-1} - 2^{\frac{n-e}{2}-1} = 6, \qquad
    -1\!: 2^{n} - 2^{n-e} - 1 = 47, \qquad
    15\!: 2^{n-e-1} + 2^{\frac{n-e}{2}-1} = 10 .
\end{equation}
$C_{1,2}$, $C_{1,3}$ and $C_{2,3}$ each give exactly 6, 47 and 10 (figure below).

\textbf{3) The pair is preferred.} Taking every fifth bit of $s_1$, i.e.\ $s_1[5n \bmod 63]$, gives $s_2$ shifted by
6 lags. So $s_2$ is the $q = 5$ decimation of $s_1$, and Gold's condition holds: $q = 2^2 + 1$ and $n/\gcd(n, 2) = 3$ is
odd. I also tried $q = 13$, 21 and 23, and none of them gives $s_2$.

\textbf{4) Sum check.} For any two $\pm 1$ sequences, $\sum_l C_{u,v}[l] = \frac{1}{N}\big(\sum_n d_u[n]\big)\big(\sum_n d_v[n]\big)$.
Each of the three sequences has 32 ones, so the sum should be $(-1)(-1)/63 = 1/63$. The counts in
Table~\ref{tab:cc} give $(6 \times (-17) + 47 \times (-1) + 10 \times 15)/63 = 1/63$. \checkmark

\textbf{5) The whole family.} The Gold family has $N + 2 = 65$ codewords: $d_1$, $d_2$, and $d_1[n]\,d_2[n+k]$ for
$k = 0, \ldots, 62$. I computed the cross-correlation for all $\binom{65}{2} = 2080$ pairs at all 63 lags, and every
value was $-17/63$, $-1/63$ or $15/63$.

$d_3$ itself is not an m-sequence, so its own autocorrelation is not two-valued. Its 62 sidelobes are
also $-17$, $-1$ and $15$ (over 63), occurring 12, 38 and 12 times. A Gold family gives up the perfect $-1/63$
autocorrelation of a single m-sequence. In return it provides 65 codewords whose cross-correlation never exceeds
$17/63$, and that is the property a CDMA system such as GPS relies on to tell users apart.
